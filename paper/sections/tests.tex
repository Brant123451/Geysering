\section{Experimental assessment}
\label{sec:tests}

The evaluation is organized as a sequence of test campaigns against published
geysering experiments. Within each campaign, individual configurations are
selected to span the experimentally observed regimes, and all configurations
are computed with the single frozen solver configuration of
Section~\ref{sec:model:numerics}. Simulations start from the documented
experimental initial conditions; the shaft motion, the air release, and the
regime selection are outcomes of the computation. No prescribed release rate,
scripted geyser height, overflow accumulator, or per-case parameter fitting
is used.

Three campaigns are selected from the experimental geysering literature so
that each contributes a different kind of evidence.
Campaign~1 (Section~\ref{sec:tests:vw2011}) reproduces the water-filled
ventilation-shaft experiments of Vasconcelos and Wright
\cite{vasconcelos2011}---the most widely cited controlled geysering
experiments and the standard benchmark for geysering models---and tests the
time-resolved histories in both regimes: Test~A in the non-geysering branch
and Test~B in the geysering branch.
Campaign~2 (Section~\ref{sec:tests:cong2017}) reproduces the
horizontal-pipe/vertical-riser experiments of Cong, Chan and Lee
\cite{cong2017}, the only campaign that condenses its outcome into an
explicit two-parameter geysering criterion; it therefore tests the model as
a regime classifier across a systematic parameter sweep rather than in
individual histories.
Campaign~3 (Section~\ref{sec:tests:liu2020}) addresses the
prototype-motivated junction-chamber experiments of Liu, Shao and Zhu
\cite{liu2020}, which connect the laboratory benchmarks to a field-scale
dropshaft geometry.
Other experimental studies---the field observations of
\cite{wright2011note}, the larger-scale releases of \cite{muller2017}
designed for three-dimensional CFD calibration, the covered-manhole geysers
of \cite{zhang2022}, the mitigation study of \cite{qian2020}, and the
vertical-pipe violent geysers of \cite{leon2018}---are used as corroborating
references rather than as reproduction targets, either because their
geometry is not controlled at the level needed for one-dimensional
comparison or because their focus (cover dynamics, mitigation devices,
flashing-driven eruption) lies outside the entrapped-air release mechanism
studied here.

\subsection{Campaign 1: water-filled ventilation shafts
(Vasconcelos and Wright, 2011)}
\label{sec:tests:vw2011}

Vasconcelos and Wright \cite{vasconcelos2011} used an apparatus comprising a
horizontal acrylic pipe of diameter $D=0.094$~m with both far ends closed, a
ventilation tower of length $L=0.610$~m and variable diameter $D_t$ mounted
on a coupling $3.516$~m from the upstream end, and a $0.546$-m-long upstream
air chamber separated from the water-filled reach by a butterfly valve
(Fig.~\ref{fig:apparatus}). The air chamber was pressurized to an initial
gauge head $H_{a0}$, the tower was partially filled to an initial level
$Y_{fs,0}$, and each run started when the valve was opened by hand in less
than one second. The pressurized pocket then propagated along the pipe crown
toward the tower and vented through the water column inside it. A pressure
transducer was located $1.07$~m downstream of the valve. The experimental
programme varied $D_t\in\{57.1,\,44.4,\,25.4,\,12.7\}$~mm
($D_t/D=0.607$--$0.135$), $H_{a0}\in\{0.305,\,0.610,\,0.915\}$~m, and
$Y_{fs,0}\in\{0.254,\,0.356,\,0.457\}$~m. The controlling variable was found
to be the tower diameter: for $D_t/D=0.607$ the free-surface rise never
exceeded 10\% of $L$, whereas for $D_t/D=0.135$ the free surface reached the
tower top in every repetition.

Two configurations are selected to span the two regimes
(Table~\ref{tab:cases}). Following \cite{vasconcelos2011}, results are
non-dimensionalized by the tower length and the tower gravity-velocity
scale: elevations and pressure heads as $Y^{*}=Y/L$ and $H^{*}=H/L$, and time
as $T_{\mathrm{rel}}^{*}=t\sqrt{gD_t}/L$. Geysering is defined as the free surface
$Y^{*}_{fs}$ reaching the tower top ($Y^{*}=1$) before the rising air--water
interface $Y^{*}_{int}$ catches the free surface.

The one-dimensional model geometry follows the apparatus exactly: upstream
air chamber $0.546$~m (the initial pocket volume,
$3.79\times10^{-3}$~m$^3$), middle pipe $2.970$~m, downstream closed pipe
$0.490$~m, tower at $x=3.516$~m, and the transducer sampled at $x=1.616$~m.
The initial pocket pressure is
$P_{a0}=P_{\mathrm{atm}}+\rho_l g H_{a0}$; the pipe downstream of the valve
and the tower below $Y_{fs,0}$ are initially at rest and water-filled. The
experimental curves used for comparison were digitized from the published
figures of \cite{vasconcelos2011} (pressure head from Figs.~5 and~6;
free-surface and interface trajectories from Figs.~7 and~8, three
experimental repetitions each); the digitized repetitions are shown either
individually or as a band.

\subsubsection{Test A: large tower, non-geysering branch
($D_t/D=0.607$)}
\label{sec:tests:vw2011:caseA}

Test~A evaluates whether the one-dimensional model selects the observed
non-geysering branch and whether its reduced tower description is consistent
with a spatially resolved calculation.  The Case-A calculation uses a
Tosan-type shock-fitted mixed-flow interface, the conservative true-dry-bed
extension, and the pressurized MOC branch also used for Test~B; only the
Case-A initial gas head and tower parameters are substituted.  This horizontal
core is coupled to the connected-pocket tower closure.
In the experiment, the pocket vents without overflow: the free surface remains
near $Y^{*}_{fs}\simeq0.6$ during the interface rise and the pressure head
retains a long plateau before pocket exhaustion \citep{vasconcelos2011}.
Both the one-dimensional calculation and the auxiliary two-dimensional
OpenFOAM calculation select this branch.

Figure~\ref{fig:caseA_snapshots} compares three complete-domain states at
identical physical times, without a time shift.  The rows span the
post-impact reflected-bore propagation at $t=1.50$~s, gas entry into the
tower at $t=6.90$~s, and vented liquid-column recession at $t=10.10$~s.
Both calculations therefore preserve the same non-geysering sequence from
horizontal-pocket propagation to tower venting and subsequent drainage.
The two-dimensional VOF field additionally resolves lateral bore and
interface deformation that the one-dimensional reconstruction cannot
represent.

The unshifted time histories in Fig.~\ref{fig:caseA_curves} provide the
quantitative comparison.  The contact-preserving release removes the
nonphysical start-up spikes seen with the mixed-flow hand-off.  Its mean
one-dimensional pressure plateau is $H^{*}=0.539$ over
$4\leq T_{\mathrm{rel}}^{*}\leq7.5$, essentially the digitized experimental
median of $0.537$.  The calculated pressure decrease is nevertheless late:
the experiment falls below $H^{*}=0.3$ at
$T_{\mathrm{rel}}^{*}=8.28$, whereas the one-dimensional trace remains near
the plateau until the late venting stage.  No time or pressure shift is
applied.  The crown-referenced two-dimensional plateau is $0.562$ after
conversion from its probe elevation; this is a hydrostatic datum conversion,
not a fitted offset.

Panel~(b) also removes the artificial flat free-surface history of the
previous diagnostic.  The one-dimensional free surface rises smoothly during
gas entry and reaches $Y^{*}_{fs,\max}=0.613$, compared with the largest
digitized experimental marker of $0.649$ and the two-dimensional maximum of
$0.623$.  The model interface lifts from the tower base at
$T_{\mathrm{rel}}^{*}=7.85$ and reaches the free surface at $9.18$; the
corresponding experimental markers begin at $7.29$ and meet at about $8.43$.
Thus the revised calculation reproduces the no-overflow branch, pressure
scale, and gradual free-surface rise, while underpredicting the early
interface-rise rate and delaying the terminal depressurization.  These
remaining differences are consistent with representing a coherent gas core
and draining wall film by a cross-section-averaged tower closure.

The planar OpenFOAM calculation is used here as a supporting spatial
diagnostic, not as a geometry-exact reference solution.  In particular, its
planar tower-to-pipe area ratio is $D_t/D=0.607$, whereas the circular
experimental apparatus has $A_t/A_p=(D_t/D)^2=0.369$; the planar model also
cannot reproduce a three-dimensional annular wall film.  Accordingly, the
experimental curves remain the basis for quantitative validation, while the
two-dimensional frames and histories test whether the reduced model follows
a physically consistent phase-evolution pathway.

\subsubsection{Test B: small tower, geysering branch
($D_t/D=0.135$)}
\label{sec:tests:vw2011:caseB}

This narrow-tower configuration provides the geysering counterpart to
Test~A.  Vasconcelos and Wright reported a geyser in every repetition: the
three Fig.~8 records first show gas in the tower over
$T_{\mathrm{rel}}^{*}=3.648$--$3.742$, while the free surface reaches the rim over
$T_{\mathrm{rel}}^{*}=3.900$--$3.981$ and therefore precedes gas breakthrough.  The
credible high-interface markers occur over $T_{\mathrm{rel}}^{*}=4.09$--$4.17$.  The
Fig.~6 transducer record has a pre-release plateau near $H^{*}=0.76$ and a
collapse envelope of approximately $T_{\mathrm{rel}}^{*}=4.02$--$4.18$
\cite{vasconcelos2011}.

The frozen one-dimensional calculation uses the crown-datum, pocket-fed,
flooding-limited junction treatment defined in
Section~\ref{sec:model:junction}; no Case-B time alignment or coefficient
adjustment is introduced.  A planar OpenFOAM calculation is included only
as an independent spatial diagnostic.  It retains the published pipe
lengths and initial heads but uses the area-equivalent slit width
$W=D_t^2/D$ and neglects surface tension.  It is therefore a controlled
two-dimensional surrogate for the release pathway, not a geometry-exact
representation of the $12.7$-mm circular tower.

Both calculations nevertheless select the observed geysering branch
(Fig.~\ref{fig:caseB_snapshots}).  In the one-dimensional result the gas
interface enters the tower at $T_{\mathrm{rel}}^{*}=3.52$ and the free surface reaches the
rim at $T_{\mathrm{rel}}^{*}=3.70$; the corresponding two-dimensional events occur at
$T_{\mathrm{rel}}^{*}=3.79$ and $4.11$.  Thus both preserve the experimental ordering---the
water surface spills before the rising gas core catches it---although the
two-dimensional surrogate develops the sequence later.  The snapshot
columns are matched by these physical stages rather than by imposing a
common clock, and each panel retains its own unshifted $T_{\mathrm{rel}}^{*}$.

Taken together, Tests A and B show that the frozen, regime-conditioned model
selects venting without overflow in the wide tower and eruption in the narrow
tower.  For Test~B the one-dimensional model most closely reproduces the
pre-eruption pressure and water-column scales, while its eruption starts
early and its blowdown ends late.  The planar calculation supports the same
entry-to-overflow morphology but overpredicts the pre-release head and water
level and delays the principal events.  Quantitative validation therefore
remains anchored to the experiment; the two-dimensional result tests spatial
consistency and exposes the limits of the planar geometric surrogate.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} relates the remaining timing pattern to the
shaft-mouth and entrainment closures.

\subsection{Campaign 2: entrapped-air release from a horizontal pipe into a
vertical riser (Cong, Chan and Lee, 2017)}
\label{sec:tests:cong2017}

\subsubsection{Experimental apparatus and test conditions}
\label{sec:tests:cong2017:setup}

The apparatus of \cite{cong2017} consists of a horizontal acrylic pipe of
diameter $D=0.05$~m and length approximately $6$~m, connected at its
upstream end to a constant-head tank and carrying a vertical riser of height
$1.8$~m and variable diameter ($D_r=16$--$46$~mm) mounted through a
tee-junction. An air pocket of prescribed initial length $L_0$ is created in
the horizontal pipe between ball valves; releasing it under the upstream
head $H_0$ drives the pocket along the pipe crown to the riser, where it
vents through the initially water-filled column. Air--water interface
trajectories in the pipe and riser were measured by video and high-speed
camera, and pressures near the pipe end and at the riser base by
transducers. From their parameter study the authors condensed the outcome
into a two-parameter geysering criterion,
\begin{equation}
\frac{D_r}{D}\le 0.62
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
V^{*}_{air}=\frac{V_{air}}{(\pi D_r^2/4)\,H_0}\ge 3.42,
\label{eq:cong_criterion}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ geysering requires both a sufficiently narrow riser and a pocket
volume large relative to the riser water column.

This campaign therefore evaluates the model in a different role from
Campaign~1: not against individual time histories, but as a regime
classifier over a systematic sweep. Two comparisons are made. First, the
seven high-speed-camera runs of the experimental Series~B
(B-H1\,--\,B-H7: $H_0=0.66$~m, $L_0=0.61$~m, riser diameters
$D_r/D=0.32$--$0.92$) are computed individually and compared against the
measured classification, pocket arrival times, and mean rise velocities.
Second, a broader synthetic sweep over the experimental parameter ranges
($D_r=16$--$46$~mm, $L_0=0.61$--$1.8$~m, $H_0=0.66$--$0.88$~m; 63
configurations) is computed and the model classification is compared
against the experimental criterion, Eq.~(\ref{eq:cong_criterion}).

All runs of this campaign use the same fully synchronous junction coupling
as Campaign~1, with the identical frozen solver configuration (the
Campaign-1 Test-B closure set; no parameter is changed between campaigns or
between runs). Two geometric elements are specific to the apparatus: the
upstream boundary is the constant-head tank, and the pocket is created
\emph{downstream} of the riser tee, so the released pocket travels upstream
along the crown toward the riser. The tee position (2.88~m from the tank)
is fixed from the experiment's own kinematics---the measured arrival times
and front speeds of Table~2 of \cite{cong2017} give a valve-to-tee distance
$T_a\,U_f$ of $2.51$/$1.92$/$1.32$~m for the three pocket lengths
$L_0=0.61$/$1.2$/$1.8$~m, all placing the tee at the same station---and is
held fixed across the sweep. An earlier version of this campaign used a
staged horizontal/vertical treatment whose classifier reduced to a static
volume--diameter criterion; those results were circular as a validation of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:cong_criterion}) and are superseded by the fully coupled
results reported here.

\subsubsection{Series B: classification, arrival times, and rise velocities}
\label{sec:tests:cong2017:seriesB}

Table~\ref{tab:cong_bh} and Fig.~\ref{fig:cong_bh} summarize the seven
Series-B runs. The model reproduces the measured classification in five of
the seven: geysering for B-H1 ($D_r/D=0.32$) and no geysering for
B-H4--B-H7 ($D_r/D=0.62$--$0.92$), with no false positives. The two
intermediate geysering runs B-H2 and B-H3 ($D_r/D=0.42$, $0.52$) are
misclassified as non-geysering: the computed free surface is driven to
$0.87$ and $0.82$~m but does not reach the rim. The miss is one-sided---the
model errs only toward under-predicting eruption---and its mechanism is
identified in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}: the volume-neutral mouth
exchange, which is what lets the narrow-riser pocket rebuild the
overpressure that powers the B-H1 geyser, also cancels part of the
one-to-one column lift in the reservoir-fed layout, and the deficit grows
with riser width. The computed arrival times are late by $1.5$~s on
average (largest for the widest riser, where the arrested crown current
covers the final reach slowly), against a measured spread of
$7.84$--$8.22$~s.

In the geysering run B-H1 the computed eruption-window climb speeds
($v_{fs}=1.71$, $v_{int}=1.28$~m/s as the fastest sustained $0.6$-s climb)
bracket the measured whole-event means ($0.92$, $1.23$~m/s): the eruption
jet itself is over-sharp, the same narrow-riser bias identified in Test~B
of Campaign~1. In the no-geysering branch the computed gas-nose climbs
($0.69$--$1.20$~m/s) exceed the measured $0.42$--$0.48$~m/s because the
computed climb concentrates in surge pulses rather than the steady
Taylor-scale ascent observed; the computed free-surface response
($0.20$--$0.65$~m/s during the pulses, zero sustained rise) shows the same
pulsed character against the measured steady $0.2$--$0.26$~m/s.

The computed pipe-end pressure transient now differentiates the branches
under the synchronous coupling: the geysering case B-H1 develops a
compression surge to $1.71\,H_0$ (cycle-averaged) against about
$1.9\,H_0$ reported, while the no-geysering case B-H6 shows only a gentle
post-arrival hump to $1.02\,H_0$ against about $1.4\,H_0$ reported. The
staged treatment gave both runs the same $2.07\,H_0$ peak; recovering the
experimental differentiation---a wide riser vents the pocket and thereby
damps the pipe-end pressure---is the principal gain of the synchronous
coupling, obtained at the cost of the two intermediate-diameter
classification misses discussed above.

\subsubsection{Regime map against the experimental criterion}
\label{sec:tests:cong2017:map}

Figure~\ref{fig:cong_map} shows the 63-configuration sweep in the criterion
plane $(D_r/D,\ V^{*}_{air})$. Each configuration is one full synchronous
simulation classified with no knowledge of Eq.~(\ref{eq:cong_criterion});
the criterion lines are drawn for comparison only. The model classification
agrees with the experimental criterion in 39 of 63 configurations, and the
24 disagreements are strictly one-sided: in every one the criterion
predicts geysering and the model does not. There are no false positives---%
every configuration the model erupts lies inside the criterion's geysering
region, and the no-geysering half-plane $D_r/D>0.62$ is classified
correctly throughout, as is the small-volume region
$V^{*}_{air}<3.42$. The misses concentrate at intermediate diameters
($D_r/D=0.42$--$0.62$) combined with large pocket volumes ($L_0\ge1.2$~m),
where the computed free surface is driven to $1.0$--$1.6$~m but falls short
of the $1.8$-m rim; seven of the 24 reach within $0.45$~m of it. The
pattern is the Series-B under-lift bias extended over the parameter plane:
the model captures the physical structure of the regime boundary---both
end members and the interaction sense that eruption requires narrow risers
\emph{and} large relative pocket volume---but places the eruption
threshold conservatively, under-predicting eruption in the transition
band. For screening use the bias direction matters: the model as frozen
errs toward missing geysers near the boundary, not toward false alarms.

\subsection{Campaign 3: stormwater geyser in a vertical shaft above a
junction chamber (Liu, Shao and Zhu, 2020)}
\label{sec:tests:liu2020}

\subsubsection{Experimental apparatus and one-dimensional representation}
\label{sec:tests:liu2020:setup}

The apparatus of \cite{liu2020} is a 1:20-scale model of a 27~m deep
Edmonton prototype dropshaft. A 5.80~m upstream pipe ($D=0.20$~m,
slope 1:100) feeds a transparent junction chamber
($0.30\times0.30\times0.45$~m) with an invert drop of $0.18$~m to a
5.95~m downstream pipe ($D_d=0.28$~m). A vertical shaft of diameter
$d_r=0.06$~m and height $1.22$~m is mounted at the chamber lid centre
and vents to the atmosphere. Inflow is imposed as a flow step
$Q_0\to Q_1$ with valve opening time $T_v\approx0.4$~s. Pressure
transducers PT1--PT4 monitor the shaft, the chamber lid (PT2, the
primary datum), the chamber floor, and the upstream crown.

Three configurations are computed with the same frozen solver as
Campaigns~1--2. The horizontal reach is represented as a single
composite one-dimensional domain---upstream pipe, chamber, and
downstream pipe---with internal momentum faces at the chamber
connections and a rigid-column ordinary differential equation for the
shaft water column coupled at the lid tap. This is a reduced
one-dimensional formulation: three-dimensional chamber mixing, slug
venting at the chamber gas port, and the arrival of a compressing
air pocket along the upstream crown in the late-time regime are not
resolved at the level of the companion two-fluid/RTRP framework and are
stated explicitly where they limit the comparison.

\subsubsection{Branch pair A2/B3: downstream boundary flips the outcome}
\label{sec:tests:liu2020:AB}

Cases A2 and B3 share the same inflow step ($Q_0=20$, $Q_1=100$~L/s) and
differ only in the downstream tail: A2 discharges to an open channel
(weir-controlled, $h_d/D_d=1/4$), whereas B3 is initially surcharged
with a submerged tail gate. In the experiment A2 never geysered while
B3 produced a single-shoot eruption on every repetition
(Fig.~5 of \cite{liu2020}).

The model reproduces this branch flip without retuning. For A2 the
riser column remains below the rim ($h_{r,\max}=0.47$~m against a
shaft height of $1.22$~m) and the steady chamber pressures agree with
the digitized Fig.~3 record: PT3 $5.26$~kPa against $4.99$~kPa
($-5\%$) and PT2 $1.80$~kPa against $2.15$~kPa ($+16\%$ in the
standing-column head that emerges from the composite domain rather than
being prescribed). For B3 the computed run geysered, with the free
surface reaching the shaft top and overflow. The eruption timing is
close---peak PT2 at $1.68$~s against $1.47$~s---but the compressive
peak amplitudes are underpredicted with the frozen elastic branch
celerity $a_{wh}=40$~m/s: PT2 peak $31$~kPa against $55$~kPa
($-44\%$). A sensitivity run at $a_{wh}=80$~m/s gives $63.5$~kPa,
confirming that the deficit scales with the acoustic wave speed rather
than with a missing branch-specific closure. The computed jet height
$h=2.17$~m lies on the experimental regression line of
Fig.~7(a) ($h=0.694\,P_{\max}/\rho g+0.309$~m, $R^2=0.97$).

\subsubsection{Case C9: transient-driven geysers and the analytic
mass-oscillation model}
\label{sec:tests:liu2020:C9}

Case C9 ($Q_0=25\to Q_1=40$~L/s, surcharged downstream tail,
initial shaft column $h_{r0}=0.30$~m, sealed air wedge on the upstream
crown in the experiment) is the authors' detailed two-stage case: the
first two geysers are driven by the hydraulic transient before the
trapped pocket reaches the chamber at $t\approx6.46$~s; geysers
3--8 are pocket-driven and lie outside the present reduced solver.

For the phase-one comparison the pocket is omitted in the computation
(the all-water variant assumed in Eq.~(7) of \cite{liu2020}), while the
sealed-pocket run is retained as a qualitative cushioning check. The
analytic head at the chamber lid is
\begin{equation}
H_s(t)=h_{r0}+\frac{L_d}{gA_d}\frac{\mathrm{d}Q_u}{\mathrm{d}t}
\left(1-\cos\frac{2\pi t}{T}\right),
\qquad
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L_d A_r}{gA_d}+\frac{h_{r0}}{g}},
\label{eq:liu_eq7}
\end{equation}
with $T=1.52$~s for C9.

Figure~\ref{fig:liu_c9_phase1} compares the digitized PT2 record of
Fig.~9 (converted to head at the chamber-top datum) against
Eq.~(\ref{eq:liu_eq7}) and the all-water model over the first
5~s. The first peak is reproduced in timing and magnitude: measured
$P_{1m}=10.69$~kPa at $t=0.50$~s, model $11.5$~kPa at $0.54$~s; the
free surface reaches the shaft top at $0.73$~s in the experiment and
$0.93$~s in the model. The oscillation period is $T=1.45$~s in the
paper (Eq.~(8)) against $1.13$~s in the model and $1.52$~s in
Eq.~(\ref{eq:liu_eq7}); the model is closer to the analytic value than
to the measured period but still fast, consistent with the stiff
one-dimensional tail-gate coupling. Steady heads after the first
transient agree within $\approx20\%$ (PT2 final $8.79$ vs $10.6$~kPa).
The sealed-pocket variant delays and cushions the first peak as in the
experiment qualitatively, then drifts after $\approx4$~s when the wedge
compresses through the reduced gas model---declared out of scope for
phase two.

Taken together, the three cases show that the composite-domain
formulation extends the frozen Campaign~1--2 solver to a
prototype-motivated junction geometry: a downstream-boundary branch
pair (A2/B3) and a transient mass-oscillation geyser (C9 phase~1) are
reproduced at the level of regime selection, timing, and---where the
physics is hydraulic rather than acoustic---amplitude. The principal
residual in this campaign is compressive under-resolution of short
pressure spikes when the frozen elastic celerity is used, and the
deliberate omission of pocket-transport physics after the pocket
arrives at the chamber.
