\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Geysering denotes the violent expulsion of air--water mixtures through
manholes, dropshafts, or ventilation shafts of stormwater and combined-sewer
systems during rapid filling events. Field observations show mixtures rising
tens of metres above ground, damaging manhole covers and endangering the
public. Early explanations based on pure inertial oscillations of the water
column were contradicted by field pressure records showing that the
piezometric head near erupting shafts never approaches the ground surface
\cite{wright2011note}; the driving mechanism is instead the release of large
entrapped air pockets through partially or fully water-filled vertical shafts
\cite{vasconcelos2011,wright2011note}. The laboratory experiments of
Vasconcelos and Wright \cite{vasconcelos2011} established this air-release
mechanism in a controlled single-shaft apparatus and remain the primary
benchmark for geysering models. Subsequent experimental programmes examined
the release of entrapped air from a horizontal pipe into a vertical shaft
\cite{cong2017}, water displacement and geysering in larger-scale shafts
\cite{muller2017}, stormwater geysers above junction chambers modelled on a
prototype dropshaft \cite{liu2020}, geysers through covered manholes
\cite{zhang2022}, and mitigation measures for storm geysers
\cite{qian2020}. The physical core of the event---a large gas pocket rising
and expanding in a vertical water column until the expansion becomes
violent---has also been isolated in the multiphase-flow literature on Taylor
bubble expansion \cite{zhouprosperetti2019}.

Numerical prediction of geysering spans two extremes. Three-dimensional
volume-of-fluid simulations reproduce individual laboratory events in detail
\cite{chan2018}, but their cost restricts them to single shafts and short
time windows, so they cannot be used for network-scale screening of hundreds
of shafts under many storm scenarios. At the other extreme, one-dimensional
transient mixed-flow models are fast enough for system-scale simulation, but
most existing formulations treat the air phase incompletely. Preissmann-slot
and two-component-pressure formulations follow the pressurization of the
liquid phase \cite{vasconcelos2006}, while entrapped air, when represented at
all, enters through lumped pocket laws, prescribed release rates, or
boundary conditions. Rigid-column two-phase models of the type used by
Vasconcelos and Wright \cite{vasconcelos2011} to interpret their own
experiments prescribe the topology of the air pocket and the water column in
advance, which limits them to geometries where that topology is known.
Because the selection between benign venting and geysering depends on the
coupled dynamics of pocket compression, air intrusion into the shaft, and
counter-current air--water exchange at the shaft mouth, a predictive
one-dimensional model must carry the gas phase as part of the computed state
rather than as a prescribed forcing.

A companion methods paper \cite{xue2026model} developed a one-dimensional
conservative finite-volume framework for transient closed-conduit mixed flows
in which the stratified reaches are described by a decoupled two-fluid system
that retains gas mass and gas momentum as conserved variables, liquid-full
reaches use an elastic-pipe water-hammer closure, and moving
pressurized--stratified fronts are treated as Regime-Transition Riemann
problems embedded in a conservative cut-cell front-tracking update. In that
framework, air-pocket compression, gas transport, and interfacial-instability
growth are outcomes of the resolved gas--liquid dynamics. The methods paper
verified the framework on interfacial-wave, slug-formation, air-pocket
pressure, and integrated mixed-flow benchmarks, and included a schematic
one-dimensional geysering-onset test; it did not, however, attempt a
quantitative comparison against geysering experiments.

The present paper provides that application-level assessment. The framework
of \cite{xue2026model} is applied, unchanged in its formulation, to
laboratory geysering experiments in which a vertical shaft is coupled to a
horizontal conduit and an entrapped air pocket is released toward the shaft.
The vertical shaft is discretized as the same one-dimensional two-fluid
system rotated to the vertical and coupled to the horizontal pipe through a
junction closure with physically motivated local losses. All regime
selection---whether the free surface reaches the shaft rim before the rising
air--water interface catches it---emerges from the computed state.

The evaluation is organized as test campaigns against published experiments,
each contributing a different kind of evidence. The first campaign is the
water-filled ventilation-shaft experiment of Vasconcelos and Wright
\cite{vasconcelos2011}, from which two configurations are selected to span
the two observed regimes: a large-tower case in the non-geysering branch and
a small-tower case in the geysering branch, testing the time-resolved
pressure and interface histories. The second campaign is the
horizontal-pipe/vertical-riser experiment of Cong, Chan and Lee
\cite{cong2017}, whose systematic parameter study condensed the geysering
outcome into an explicit two-parameter criterion; it tests the model as a
regime classifier across the high-speed-camera series and a 63-configuration
sweep of the experimental parameter ranges. All computations use a frozen
solver configuration and start from the experimental initial conditions,
without prescribed release rates, scripted geyser heights, or per-case
parameter fitting. A third campaign against the prototype-motivated
junction-chamber experiments of Liu, Shao and Zhu \cite{liu2020} tests
branch selection with a downstream tail boundary, eruption height against
peak pressure, and the transient mass-oscillation model of their Series~C
cases; other experimental studies
\cite{muller2017,zhang2022,qian2020,leon2018} serve as corroborating
references.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:model} summarizes the decoupled two-fluid framework and
describes the vertical-shaft branch and junction closures used for geysering
configurations. Section~\ref{sec:tests} presents the test cases and the
comparison with the experiments. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the
capabilities and limitations identified by the comparison, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes.
