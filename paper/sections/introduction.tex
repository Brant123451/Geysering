\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Geysering is the rapid expulsion of an air--water mixture through a manhole,
dropshaft, or ventilation shaft during transient filling of a drainage
system. The resulting jet can displace covers, damage nearby infrastructure,
and endanger the public. Field pressure records rule out a purely inertial
water-column explanation because the piezometric head near an erupting shaft
need not reach ground level \cite{wright2011note}. Controlled experiments
instead identify the release and expansion of a large entrapped air pocket
through a partly or fully water-filled shaft as the principal driving
mechanism \cite{vasconcelos2011,wright2011note}.

Laboratory studies have documented several manifestations of this mechanism.
Vasconcelos and Wright \cite{vasconcelos2011} isolated air-pocket release in
a single ventilation shaft and documented both benign venting and geysering.
Cong et al.\ \cite{cong2017} varied riser diameter and initial pocket volume
systematically and proposed a two-parameter regime criterion. Later studies
examined larger shafts \cite{muller2017}, prototype-motivated junction
chambers \cite{liu2020}, covered manholes \cite{zhang2022}, and mitigation
measures \cite{qian2020}. Related multiphase-flow work has also isolated the
rapid expansion of a Taylor bubble in a vertical liquid column
\cite{zhouprosperetti2019}. These studies indicate that pressure alone does not
determine geysering. The outcome depends on pocket compression, gas delivery to
the shaft, counter-current exchange, and the time available for the water
column to reach the rim.

Numerical approaches resolve this competition at different levels of detail.
Three-dimensional volume-of-fluid simulations can reproduce the local
interface and jet structure of an individual event \cite{chan2018}. Their
computational cost limits application to network-scale studies with many
shafts and rainfall scenarios. One-dimensional mixed-flow models are more
practical at that scale. Conventional Preissmann-slot and
two-component-pressure formulations capture transitions between free-surface
and pressurized flow but do not, by themselves, conserve and transport a
distinct gas phase \cite{vasconcelos2006}. Entrapped gas is therefore commonly
introduced through a lumped pocket law, a specified release rate, or a
boundary condition. Rigid-column two-phase models likewise require the
gas-pocket and water-column topology to be known in advance
\cite{vasconcelos2011}. These approaches can represent a selected apparatus
once the gas topology or release has been specified. They cannot independently
determine whether the computed gas--liquid dynamics produce venting or
geysering.

A companion methods paper \cite{xue2026model} addressed this modelling gap for
transient mixed flow in closed conduits. The stratified branch conserves gas
mass and momentum within a decoupled two-fluid system, and the liquid-full
branch uses an elastic-pipe closure. Moving pressurized--stratified interfaces
are treated as regime-transition Riemann problems and tracked by a conservative
cut-cell update. The restoring coefficient also retains interfacial-shear
slip, so loss of hyperbolicity identifies the onset of inviscid
Kelvin--Helmholtz instability. This is an onset diagnostic rather than a
closure for subsequent wave breaking or gas entrainment. The methods paper
verified the numerical formulation with canonical mixed-flow tests and
included a schematic geyser-onset example, but did not assess the model
quantitatively against geysering experiments.

Here, we assess case-specific one-dimensional realizations derived from the
companion framework against geysering experiments. The realizations share
conservative phase inventories and connected-pocket accounting, while their
regular-face fluxes, shaft reductions, and junction closures follow the needs
of each apparatus. Their parameters and numerical settings are fixed before comparison,
apart from the disclosed in-sample Test~B screen. The C9 phase-one all-water
variant is identified separately because it follows the analytic comparison in
the source study. Gas release, water-column displacement, and regime selection
are therefore computed outcomes. No measured release history or geyser
trajectory is imposed.

The assessment draws on three complementary evidence sets. The first campaign
compares time-resolved pressure, interface, and free-surface histories for the
non-geysering and geysering ventilation-shaft tests of
Vasconcelos and Wright \cite{vasconcelos2011}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:apparatus}). The second treats the model as
a regime classifier against the seven filmed riser tests of Cong et al.\
\cite{cong2017}; a separate 63-configuration simulation sweep is compared with
their empirical regime criterion. The third examines the junction-chamber
experiments of Liu et al.\ \cite{liu2020}, including a change in geysering
outcome caused by the downstream boundary and a transient mass-oscillation
event. Together, the campaigns examine whether the model reproduces the
observed outcome, whether that outcome pattern is consistent with an empirical
regime map, and whether the reduced representation predicts the initial
response of a prototype-motivated chamber.

This study therefore examines when gas-conserving one-dimensional
realizations reproduce observed geysering. It also identifies when
unresolved junction, liquid-film, and pressure-wave processes control the
error. Sections~\ref{sec:model} and~\ref{sec:model:numerics} summarize the
governing formulation and numerical method, including the application-specific
closures. Section~\ref{sec:tests} reports the three experimental comparisons.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets the common successes and limitations,
and Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} states the resulting scope of use.
