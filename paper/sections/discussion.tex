\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Comparison across the three experimental data sets}

Each data set stresses a different part of the model. Campaign~1 examines
event evolution, Campaign~2 examines flow-regime classification, and
Campaign~3 examines the response to the downstream chamber boundary.
Campaigns~1--2 show that explicit gas conservation and transport can recover
the observed pattern of venting versus geysering. Campaign~3 instead tests the
reduced hydraulic branches against a boundary-driven regime change and an
initial all-water transient. Matching these outcomes does not guarantee
accurate event timing, interface velocity, or short-duration pressure peaks.

In Campaign~1, the model selects the observed non-geysering outcome for the
wide tower and the geysering outcome for the narrow tower. The wide-tower
evidence comes from two separate calculations. The visualization-only
reconstruction preserves the non-overflow event order, but its tower empties
faster than the planar calculation in the last common frame. The independently
identified connected-pocket sensitivity gives a pressure plateau of
$H^*=0.539$, while the extracted experimental central trace gives $0.537$.
Its maximum free-surface elevation is $Y^*_{\mathrm{fs},\max}=0.613$; the
largest experimental marker is $0.649$, and the planar value is $0.623$.
The interface lift-off and free-surface catch times are 7.85 and 9.18,
respectively. The corresponding three-run spans are approximately 7.36--7.94
and 8.85--9.19. Both event times therefore remain within the digitized
scatter, although the calculation misses the fastest interface rise. These
comparisons support the non-geysering outcome and the bulk pressure and
water-level scales, but they do not show that one baseline simulation recovers
every spatial and temporal observable. The late terminal depressurization and
remaining shape differences are consistent with unresolved exchange between
the coherent pocket and the draining film. This sensitivity alone cannot
identify a unique mechanism.

The narrow tower exposes a more localized error. The selected
tracked-front calculation reproduces the pre-eruption stand ($0.834L$ versus
$0.823L$), the pressure plateau, and the geysering sequence. Gas first enters
the tower at $T_{\mathrm{rel}}^{*}=3.622$ rather than the experimental
$3.648$--$3.742$. The one-cell grid-complete rim event occurs at 3.923, within
the measured 3.900--3.981 span, and the displayed pressure falls below
$H^*=0.30$ at 4.097, within the 4.02--4.18 collapse envelope. The main
residual is instead the early/high shock-fitted front (RMSE $0.206$, bias
$+0.172$), which catches the free surface at 4.051. Yet the connected cellwise
$\alpha_g=0.50$ field front reaches only $0.016L$. The reported
$Y_{\mathrm{int}}$ must therefore be interpreted as a reduced tracked-front
closure, not a resolved dense-core isocontour. Diameter-group velocities
reported in the source provide contextual scales rather than Case-B-specific
error measures.

Campaign~2 reveals the consequence of this timing and exchange error for
flow-regime classification. The model agrees with 5 of the 7 filmed Series-B
outcomes and with the empirical criterion in 39 of the 63 simulated
configurations. It recovers the geysering end member, the non-geysering regions
with wide risers or small pockets, and the measured difference in pipe-end
pressure response. All 24 criterion-based disagreements have the same sign:
the criterion predicts geysering, whereas the model does not. This pattern is
consistent with insufficient net water-column lift near the criterion
boundary. It is not conservative for hazard screening because the model tends
to miss geysering near that boundary rather than overpredict it.

Campaign~3 tests the role of the downstream boundary. Changing only this
boundary reverses the outcome between A2 and B3, and the model captures that
reversal. At the computed B3 peak pressure of 30.84~kPa, the experimental
pressure--height regression gives approximately 2.50~m; the computed jet
height is 2.17~m. The fixed pressure-wave celerity also underpredicts the short
pressure peak (30.84 versus 55.03~kPa). For C9, the computed first pressure
peak is $11.49$~kPa at $0.54$~s, compared with $10.69$~kPa at $0.50$~s. The
predicted oscillation period is shorter than measured. Later pocket-driven
geysers lie outside the composite one-dimensional representation. The chamber
model therefore captures the initial transient response but not the later
gas-pocket dynamics.

\subsection{Capabilities of the reduced model}

For the entrapped-air comparisons in Campaigns~1--2, the model determines the
geysering outcome without a prescribed gas-release history. Pocket pressure
evolves from the computed gas mass and volume; the coupled solution then
determines gas transport into the shaft and water displacement. The response
consequently changes with riser diameter and pocket volume. Campaign~3 provides
a separate test of the reduced hydraulic branches through the downstream
boundary and the initial all-water transient. In the entrapped-air cases, a
liquid-only calculation could match a selected trace only by prescribing the
gas-side forcing and therefore could not determine the outcome independently.

The comparisons also show which quantities are reproduced most consistently at
one-dimensional resolution. Slowly varying pressure plateaus, quasi-static
water-column levels, maximum free-surface elevations, and the first
mass-oscillation peak agree more closely than interface breakthrough,
annular-film motion, and short pressure peaks. Gas inventory, liquid-column
inertia, and hydrostatic balance primarily control the former quantities. The
latter depend more strongly on local three-dimensional exchange and unresolved
time scales at the junction and shaft wall.

\subsection{Closure limitations revealed by the experiments}

The shaft-mouth closure remains the main limitation. In the narrow-tower
configuration, the resolved driven regime activates a crown-referenced
pressure datum and a pocket-fed gas core. It also activates conserved
moving-front and slug dynamics, a glug resistance, and a finite mouth aperture.
The Case-B resistance and aperture pair was chosen in the disclosed bounded
in-sample screen. Together, these
elements form a regime-conditioned closure rather than a unified
cross-sectional junction solution. The wide tower uses an invert-referenced
datum, whereas the narrow tower requires the physical crown connection. A
future junction model should transmit the vertical pressure distribution
across the mouth instead of reducing it to a single scalar datum.

A second limitation concerns the coupling between cavity propagation and vent
feed. The fixed cavity-front celerity places the initial arrivals close to the
observed scatter, but the same reduced feed slows the pocket-fed core and
delays breakthrough. Separating front speed from the pressure-driven gas flux
through the mouth would allow the two constraints to be evaluated
independently.

A third limitation is the volume-neutral counter-current exchange used at the
tee. This exchange preserves pocket pressure during balanced venting and is
needed to produce the B-H1 geyser. In reservoir-fed cases with wider risers,
however, it cancels part of the water-column displacement caused by entering
gas. Removing the exchange restores lift in those cases, but it also removes
the B-H1 pressure rebuild. Both branches therefore require a flux-limited
exchange law based on local counter-current capacity rather than a fixed
equality of gas and liquid volumes.

Dimensional reduction introduces two further limits. The fixed Taylor-core
closure does not resolve the evolving connected-pocket topology or the
draining wall film in the wide tower. The reduced chamber and wave celerity do
not reproduce the shortest B3 pressure peak or the later arrival and venting
of pockets in C9. Addressing these discrepancies requires targeted closure
development or local multidimensional coupling, not adjustment of a global
parameter to individual tests.

\subsection{Implications and scope of use}

The present evidence supports using the model to examine mechanisms and
system-scale scenarios away from the empirical criterion boundary. One
computational description tracks gas inventory, pocket compression, the
geysering outcome, and the bulk pressure and water-column response. The evidence
does not support stand-alone safety classification near the boundary. Every
Campaign~2 criterion disagreement is a missed geysering case, and the local B3
pressure peak remains sensitive to the pressure-wave representation.

The reported accuracy applies only to the tested configurations and the stated
regime-conditioned junction closures. Visualization-only and sensitivity
results retain the narrower roles defined in their captions. These comparisons
do not establish applicability across shaft shapes, manhole covers, network
interactions, or prototype Reynolds and Weber numbers. The next validation
step should use an independent apparatus without changing the closure
parameters. Simultaneous measurements of gas flux, liquid return flow, pocket
pressure, and interface topology at the shaft mouth are the priority. Such
data could distinguish an exchange-law error from a shaft-entrainment error
and make the next closure revision falsifiable.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This study assessed case-specific one-dimensional realizations of a
gas-conserving mixed-flow framework against three published experimental data
sets. Horizontal conduits and vertical shafts were coupled with
apparatus-appropriate reductions, and the gas-release history was computed rather than
prescribed. For the two ventilation-shaft cases, the corresponding realizations selected the
observed non-geysering and geysering outcomes. In the wide tower, the separately
identified connected-pocket sensitivity recovered the pressure and maximum
water-level scales. Its interface event times remained within the three-run
spread, but terminal depressurization was late. In the narrow tower, the
selected tracked-front calculation recovered the pre-eruption pressure and
water-level scales. Grid-complete rim contact and pressure collapse fell within
the measured intervals, although the reduced interface trajectory remained
early and high.

For the horizontal-pipe/vertical-riser data set, the model agreed with 5 of 7
filmed outcomes and with the empirical criterion in 39 of 63 simulated
configurations. In all 24 criterion-based disagreements, the criterion
predicted geysering and the model did not. For the junction-chamber data set,
the model recovered the A2/B3 change in geysering outcome and the first C9
pressure transient. It underpredicted the short B3 pressure peak and did not
represent the later pocket-driven events.

For Campaigns~1--2, explicit gas conservation and transport recover the main
geysering outcome patterns, bulk pressure levels, and water-column response.
Campaign~3 adds evidence that the reduced hydraulic formulation captures the
boundary-driven A2/B3 branch flip and the first C9 transient. The remaining
errors are associated with shaft-mouth exchange,
connected gas-core and wall-film dynamics, pressure-wave representation, and
chamber reduction. These realizations are therefore suitable for mechanistic assessment
away from the empirical criterion boundary. Safety-critical classification
near that boundary requires a junction model that conserves phase fluxes across
the full cross section and resolves entrainment.
