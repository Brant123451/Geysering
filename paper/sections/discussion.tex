\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Evidence across the three campaigns}

The three campaigns test the model at different levels. Campaign~1 asks
whether the computed gas and liquid fields reproduce the sequence within an
individual event. Campaign~2 asks whether those fields select the correct
branch across changes in shaft diameter and pocket volume. Campaign~3 asks
whether the same reduced description remains informative when a junction
chamber and downstream boundary participate in the transient. Considered
together, the results show that conserving and transporting the gas phase is
enough to recover the principal mechanism across all three apparatuses. They
also show that correct branch selection does not guarantee accurate event
timing, interface speed, or pressure peaks.

In Campaign~1, the wide and narrow towers occupy different experimental
branches, and the model separates them correctly. For the wide tower, the
computed maximum free-surface level is $0.66L$, compared with $0.63L$ in the
experiment. The interface velocity is $0.41\sqrt{gD}$, compared with
$0.39\sqrt{gD}$. The computed pressure plateau is also close to the measured
level. These agreements indicate that the resolved pocket volume and
hydrostatic resistance set the bulk response correctly. The remaining error
is topological. The experiment contains a coherent gas pocket with a draining
wall film, whereas the present grid-scale closure transports the shaft gas as
a dispersed phase. It therefore misses the gradual free-surface creep and
delays the pressure decay until breakthrough. After breakthrough, the local
transducer state is repeatedly re-pressurized by the elastic horizontal
water-column--pocket mode. The moving mean converts this oscillatory release
into a gradual tail, but it does not cause the late onset.

The narrow tower exposes a different error. The model reproduces the
pre-eruption stand ($0.84L$ versus $0.82L$), the pressure plateau, the rise to
the rim, and the free-surface speed ($0.39\sqrt{gD}$ versus
$0.44\sqrt{gD}$). However, the interface speed is only
 $0.71\sqrt{gD}$, compared with $1.43\sqrt{gD}$. The free surface reaches the
 rim early ($t^{*}=3.70$ versus $3.90$--$3.98$), whereas the interface reaches
 $0.85L$ late ($t^{*}=4.44$ versus the credible high-interface cluster at
 $4.09$--$4.17$). The error is therefore a stretched
gas-release sequence, not a uniform shift of all events. This distinction is
important because it localizes the deficiency to the transfer of gas through
the shaft mouth and the pocket-fed core rather than to the initial pocket
arrival alone.

Campaign~2 shows how this timing and exchange error affects regime
classification. The model captures 5 of the 7 filmed Series-B cases and 39
of the 63 configurations in the full parameter sweep. It reproduces the
geysering end member, all wide-riser and small-pocket non-geysering end
members, and the measured branch differentiation of pipe-end pressure. The
24 sweep disagreements are all experimental geysers that remain below the
rim in the computation. No computed case produces a false geyser. This
one-sided pattern is mechanistically consistent with insufficient net water-
column lift near the transition band. It should not be described as
``conservative'' for hazard screening, because missed eruptions are
non-conservative from a safety perspective.

Campaign~3 provides a complementary boundary-condition test. Changing only
the downstream condition reverses the outcome between A2 and B3, and the
model reproduces that reversal. It also places the B3 jet height on the
experimental pressure--height trend. The frozen wave speed under-resolves
the short pressure peak, yielding $30.84$~kPa rather than $55.03$~kPa. For
C9, the computed first pressure peak is $11.49$~kPa at $0.54$~s, compared
with $10.69$~kPa at $0.50$~s. The predicted oscillation period is shorter
than measured, and later pocket-driven geysers lie outside the composite
one-dimensional representation. Thus, the chamber reduction retains the
first transient response but not the full later-time gas-pocket dynamics.

\subsection{Where the reduced model succeeds}

The strongest result is the recovery of branch physics without prescribing
a gas-release history. In each campaign, the pocket pressure evolves from
the conserved gas mass and computed volume. Gas transport into the shaft and
water displacement then follow from the coupled solution. This structure
allows the model to distinguish benign venting from eruption and to respond
to changes in riser diameter, pocket volume, and downstream boundary. A
liquid-only model with a prescribed pressure or discharge could reproduce a
selected trace, but it would not provide the same independent regime test.

The results also identify which observables are robust at one-dimensional
resolution. Slowly varying pressure plateaus, quasi-static water-column
stands, maximum free-surface levels, and the first mass-oscillation peak are
captured more reliably than interface breakthrough, annular-film motion, or
short acoustic spikes. These robust quantities are governed mainly by gas
inventory, liquid-column inertia, and hydrostatic balance. The less robust
quantities depend on local three-dimensional exchange or unresolved time
scales at the junction and shaft wall.

\subsection{Closure limitations revealed by the experiments}

The dominant limitation is the shaft-mouth closure. In the narrow-tower
configuration, a crown-referenced pressure datum, a pocket-fed gas core, and
a Wallis-type flooding limit are activated by the resolved driven regime.
These elements use geometry and literature-scale constants rather than
case-specific calibration. Nevertheless, they remain a regime-conditioned
closure rather than a unified cross-sectional junction solution. The wide
tower uses an invert-referenced datum, while the narrow tower requires the
physical crown connection. A future junction model should transmit the
vertical pressure distribution across the mouth instead of reducing it to a
single scalar datum.

The second limitation is the coupling between cavity propagation and vent
feed. The frozen dissipative cavity speed places the initial arrivals within
the observed scatter. The same reduced feed then slows the pocket-fed core
and delays breakthrough. Separating the front speed from the pressure-driven
gas flux through the mouth would allow the two constraints to be satisfied
independently.

The third limitation is the volume-neutral counter-current exchange used at
the tee. This exchange preserves pocket pressure during balanced venting and
is needed to produce the B-H1 geyser. In reservoir-fed, wider-riser cases,
however, it cancels part of the water-column displacement produced by
entering gas. Removing the exchange restores lift in those cases but also
removes the B-H1 pressure rebuild. The two branches therefore require a
flux-limited exchange law based on local counter-current capacity, not a
fixed equality of gas and liquid volumes.

Two additional limits follow from dimensional reduction. A dispersed two-
fluid state does not preserve the coherent Taylor-pocket topology and its
draining film in the wide tower. A reduced chamber and softened acoustic
celerity do not resolve the shortest B3 pressure spike or the later arrival
and venting of pockets in C9. These discrepancies should be addressed by
targeted closure development or local multidimensional coupling. They should
not be hidden by adjusting a global parameter to individual tests.

\subsection{Implications and scope of use}

The present evidence supports using the framework to study mechanisms and to
screen system-scale scenarios away from the regime boundary. It can track
gas inventory, pocket compression, branch selection, and the bulk pressure
and water-column response within one computational description. The evidence
does not support using the current formulation as a stand-alone safety
classifier near the boundary. Its Campaign~2 errors are missed geysers, and
the local pressure peak in B3 is sensitive to acoustic representation.

The reported accuracy applies to the frozen numerical configuration and the
documented, regime-conditioned junction closures. It does not establish
universality across shaft shapes, manhole covers, network interactions, or
prototype Reynolds and Weber numbers. The next validation step should
therefore target an independent apparatus without modifying the closure
parameters. Priority measurements are simultaneous gas flux, liquid return
flow, pocket pressure, and interface topology at the shaft mouth. Such data
would distinguish an exchange-law error from a shaft-entrainment error and
would make the next closure revision falsifiable.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This study assessed a gas-conserving one-dimensional mixed-flow framework
against three published geysering campaigns. The model represented the shaft
and horizontal conduit with coupled two-fluid states and computed the gas
release rather than prescribing it. Across the ventilation-shaft tests, it
selected both the non-geysering and geysering branches and reproduced the
principal pressure and water-column scales. In the wide tower, the pressure
plateau was reproduced, but blowdown was delayed until breakthrough and
relaxed too gradually. The narrow-tower comparison revealed a stretched
release sequence: shaft arrival was early, whereas interface breakthrough
was late and the computed interface velocity was approximately one half of
the measured value.

Across the horizontal-pipe/vertical-riser campaign, the model classified 5
of 7 filmed cases and 39 of 63 parameter-sweep configurations. All 24 sweep
errors were missed experimental geysers near the transition band, with no
false geysers. In the junction-chamber campaign, it reproduced the A2/B3
branch reversal and the first C9 pressure transient, but under-resolved the
short B3 pressure peak and did not represent later pocket-driven events.

The combined evidence separates a useful system-scale capability from its
current limits. Resolved gas conservation and transport recover the dominant
regime mechanism, bulk pressure levels, and water-column response. Remaining
errors concentrate in shaft-mouth exchange, coherent-film topology, and
acoustic/chamber reduction. The framework is therefore suitable for
mechanistic assessment and qualified screening away from the boundary. A
unified, flux-limited junction and entrainment closure is required before the
model can be relied upon for safety-critical classification of marginal
geysering cases.
